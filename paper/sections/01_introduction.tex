\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Statistical arbitrage has been a core quantitative strategy on Wall Street since Nunzio Tartaglia's group at Morgan Stanley first automated pairs trading in the mid-1980s. The basic premise is straightforward: if two stocks share similar fundamental drivers or factor risks, their relative prices should stay tied together over time \citep{gatev2006pairs,vidyamurthy2004pairs}. When short-term order flow or sudden liquidity shocks push the spread between them too far apart, quantitative traders step in, buying the beaten-down stock and shorting the elevated one, betting that normal economic relationships will reassert themselves \citep{avellaneda2010statistical,krauss2017statistical,gu2020empirical}.

Yet empirical finance has repeatedly shown that paper backtests can be deceiving. Anomalies that look pristine in frictionless backtests often deteriorate rapidly in live trading \citep{mclean2016does,hou2020replicating}. For pairs trading in particular, \citet{do2010does} and \citet{do2012are} discovered that post-2002 returns in the US fell dramatically once realistic short-borrowing fees and trade friction were factored in. \citet{novymarx2016taxonomy} and \citet{frazzini2018trading} came to a similar conclusion across a broad spectrum of quantitative signals: strategies that rely on rapid rebalancing suffer disproportionately from transaction costs.

Almost everything we know about statistical arbitrage comes from developed markets, primarily the US. In those environments, commissions are negligible, borrow markets are deep, and liquidity rebates cushion transaction costs. Emerging markets operate under a completely different set of rules.

This study looks at how statistical arbitrage actually behaves on the National Stock Exchange of India (NSE) across the single-stock Futures \& Options (F\&O) universe over a 16-year horizon (January 2010 to December 2025). India provides a fascinating empirical testing ground for two institutional reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item You cannot easily short stocks in India's cash market. The formal Securities Lending and Borrowing (SLB) system has very little volume, so any fund manager who wants two-sided market neutrality has to trade single-stock futures contracts instead.
    \item Unlike US exchanges where fees can be negotiated down with volume, Indian derivatives carry compulsory government taxes and statutory levies. These include the Securities Transaction Tax (STT) \citep{bose2007securities}, state stamp duty, GST, exchange charges, and SEBI regulatory fees.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What the Data Actually Shows}
The core takeaway from this study is clear: \textbf{mean-reverting signals exist in Indian derivative stocks with high statistical confidence, but non-negotiable taxes and market friction destroy every rupee of theoretical profit.}

Looking across the three strategy architectures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strategy A (Gatev et al.\ 2006 Distance Pairs):} Produces a gross Sharpe of $+0.95$ ($t = +4.11$, $p < 0.001$) before fees. Once institutional transaction costs are applied, the net Sharpe collapses to $-2.52$, translating to an annualized net loss of $-25.16\%$.
    \item \textbf{Strategy B (Avellaneda \& Lee 2010 PCA-OU Reversion):} Delivers a strong gross Sharpe of $+1.74$ ($t = +7.49$, $p < 0.001$). Factoring in actual dated costs drags the net Sharpe down to $-1.63$ ($t = -7.00$), leaving the portfolio with a $-16.75\%$ annual loss.
    \item \textbf{Strategy C (Walk-Forward Machine Learning):} Achieves a gross Sharpe of $+1.00$ ($t = +4.05$, $p < 0.001$). Because machine learning algorithms pick up faster, transient mispricings, they churn the portfolio 34\% harder, pushing the net Sharpe to $-2.96$ ($t = -12.00$, with an annual net loss of $-25.91\%$).
\end{itemize}

On average, a trader would need transaction costs to fall to $0.38\times$ of standard institutional levels just to break even. That is impossible under Indian law, because statutory taxes set an inescapable baseline fee. Adjusting for multiple testing and the Deflated Sharpe Ratio \citep{bailey2014deflated,harvey2016and,benjamini1995controlling}, zero out of twelve pre-registered variations survive. The bottom line: standalone daily statistical arbitrage cannot make money in Indian single-stock F\&O, but these mean-reversion signals still offer genuine economic value as execution timing tools for institutions that already need to trade \citep{novymarx2016taxonomy}.

\subsection{Theoretical Foundations and Literature Review}
The theoretical foundation of statistical arbitrage rests on the law of one price and the concept of relative pricing efficiency in financial markets. In classical efficient market theory (Fama 1970), price divergences between fundamentally substitute assets should be fleeting and negligible. However, modern limits-to-arbitrage models (Shleifer \& Vishny 1997) demonstrate that real-world frictions, idiosyncratic noise trader risk, and capital constraints frequently prevent arbitrageurs from instantaneously enforcing fundamental parity. When institutional capital is constrained or when order flow generates temporary liquidity imbalances, prices decouple from their long-term equilibrium, creating mean-reverting displacement patterns that quantitative strategies seek to exploit.

Empirical research on statistical arbitrage began systematically with pairs trading in US equity markets. The seminal framework established by Gatev, Goetzmann, \& Rouwenhorst (2006) introduced the minimum distance method, demonstrating that pairs of stocks selected on historical normalized return proximity generated annualized excess returns exceeding 11\% between 1962 and 2002. Vidyamurthy (2004) formalised this intuition using Engle-Granger cointegration, arguing that economic ties between firms enforce a stationary linear combination of asset prices. Huck \& Afawubo (2015) and Krauss (2017) extended this literature across European and global markets, showing that while simple distance metrics capture co-movement, cointegration filters provide greater statistical robustness against structural breaks in underlying firm fundamentals.

Despite these early empirical successes, a prominent strand of modern asset pricing literature documents the rapid decay of quantitative anomalies over the past two decades. McLean \& Pontiff (2016) analyze 97 cross-sectional anomalies and find that average portfolio returns decline by roughly 58\% following academic publication, driven by competitive institutional capital chasing published alpha. In the specific context of pairs trading, Do \& Faff (2010, 2012) find that gross profitability in the US market diminished significantly post-2002. More critically, they demonstrate that once realistic short-sale borrowing fees, margin requirements, and execution costs are accounted for, net returns fall to statistically insignificant levels. Hou, Xue, \& Zhang (2020) replicate 452 anomalies and similarly conclude that the vast majority fail to clear standard hurdles of statistical significance once microcaps and realistic transaction costs are enforced.

To overcome the limitations of pairwise distance matching, Avellaneda \& Lee (2010) pioneered high-dimensional residual statistical arbitrage. By projecting cross-sectional returns onto systemic risk factors extracted via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or multi-factor models, their methodology isolates idiosyncratic asset returns. These idiosyncratic residuals are then modeled as mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stochastic processes, providing explicit estimates of the equilibrium spread, speed of mean reversion, and expected absorption time. Recent advances have sought to enhance residual signal extraction using machine learning. Gu, Kelly, \& Xiu (2020) show that non-linear tree ensembles and deep neural networks improve predictive power for asset returns by capturing complex macroeconomic interactions. Guijarro-Ordonez, Pelger, \& Gregori (2021) directly apply deep autoencoders and recurrent architectures to statistical arbitrage, finding enhanced in-sample predictability.

However, Novy-Marx \& Velikov (2016) and Frazzini, Israel, \& Moskowitz (2018) caution that algorithmic strategies characterized by high turnover suffer disproportionately from transaction costs. While high-capacity anomaly signals like value or momentum survive institutional trading costs, high-frequency mean-reversion signals experience catastrophic alpha decay. In developed markets, institutional commissions are often fractions of a basis point, execution algos leverage dark pools and crossing networks, and market makers receive maker-taker exchange rebates. Emerging markets, by contrast, present a fundamentally hostile market microstructure characterized by compulsory statutory transaction taxes, exchange turnover fees, and illiquid borrowing infrastructure. This study bridges this crucial gap in the literature by evaluating statistical arbitrage across the National Stock Exchange of India over a comprehensive 16-year horizon.
