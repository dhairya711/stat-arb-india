\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Statistical arbitrage has stood for four decades as one of the central paradigms in empirical asset pricing and quantitative portfolio management. In their seminal work, \citet{gatev2006pairs} documented substantial and persistent excess returns to a non-parametric distance-based pairs trading strategy on US equities over the 1962--2002 period, reporting annualized excess returns of up to 11\% on committed capital. Subsequent literature generalized the pairwise framework into multidimensional factor residual space, notably through the statistical factor decomposition and Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) mean-reversion formulation of \citet{avellaneda2010statistical}, and more recently via deep learning and walk-forward machine learning architectures \citep{guijarro2021deep}.

While the theoretical literature surrounding mean-reverting equity strategies is extensive, the vast majority of published studies focus on developed Western markets---chiefly the United States---where market microstructure is characterized by ultra-low transactional friction, maker-taker exchange rebates, and costless stock borrowing mechanisms. Emerging markets present an entirely distinct institutional landscape.

In this paper, we conduct an exhaustive, pre-registered empirical investigation into whether statistical arbitrage anomalies survive in the National Stock Exchange of India (NSE) single-stock Futures \& Options (F\&O) segment over the 16-year period from January 1, 2010 to December 31, 2025. The Indian equity derivatives market serves as a particularly illuminating laboratory for two reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item It is one of the highest-volume derivative markets in the world, yet single-stock short selling in the cash segment is severely restricted, forcing institutional market-neutral managers to operate almost exclusively in single-stock futures contracts.
    \item Unlike US equities, Indian derivative transactions are subject to a statutory, non-negotiable tax schedule, including the Securities Transaction Tax (STT), Stamp Duty, Goods and Services Tax (GST), exchange turnover charges, and SEBI regulatory levies.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Key Empirical Findings and the "Gross Alpha Illusion"}
Our central finding is striking: \textbf{Gross statistical arbitrage signals exist in Indian F\&O equities with high statistical significance, but they are completely annihilated by market microstructure friction.}

Across all three methodological families examined:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strategy A (Gatev et al. 2006 Distance Pairs):} Generates a gross annualized Sharpe ratio of $+0.95$ ($t$-stat $= +4.11$, $p < 0.001$). Under our institutional baseline cost model, the net Sharpe collapses to $-2.50$ with an annualized net loss of $-5.4\%$.
    \item \textbf{Strategy B (Avellaneda \& Lee 2010 PCA-OU Reversion):} Achieves a gross annualized Sharpe ratio of $+1.28$ ($t$-stat $= +7.49$, $p < 0.001$). After dated transaction costs, the net Sharpe collapses to $-1.62$ ($t$-stat $= -7.00$).
    \item \textbf{Strategy C (ML-Enhanced Walk-Forward Residuals):} Demonstrates the highest gross Sharpe of $+1.54$ ($t$-stat $= +4.05$). However, because non-linear machine learning models induce higher turnover, the net Sharpe drops to $-2.96$ ($t$-stat $= -12.00$).
\end{itemize}

The breakeven cost multiplier across all strategies averages $0.38\times$ of baseline institutional costs. Consequently, an institutional fund would need to reduce total transaction costs by more than 62\% below statutory minimums to achieve non-negative net returns. We conclude that daily statistical arbitrage in Indian single-stock F\&O is not viable as an independent, standalone alpha generator, but retains substantial economic value when repurposed as an execution timing overlay.
