\section{Indian Market Microstructure and Institutional Costs}
\label{sec:microstructure}

Trading statistical arbitrage in India requires navigating a fundamentally different market structure than in North America or Western Europe. Single-stock short selling in the Indian cash equity market is subject to strict Securities Lending and Borrowing (SLB) limitations, characterized by negligible liquidity and high borrow fees. Consequently, institutional quant funds implement long/short market-neutral strategies almost entirely within the National Stock Exchange (NSE) Futures segment.

\subsection{Dated Statutory Fee Architecture}
Unlike jurisdictions with negotiated or zero-commission fee tiers, Indian futures transactions incur statutory government taxes and exchange fees that apply to all institutional participants \citep{bose2007securities}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Securities Transaction Tax (STT):} Imposed exclusively on the sell side of futures transactions. Historically fixed at 0.0125\% ($1.25$ bps), STT was raised by 60\% to 0.0200\% ($2.00$ bps) effective October 1, 2024, under SEBI regulatory reforms aimed at cooling retail derivative speculation.
    \item \textbf{Stamp Duty:} Standardized nationwide under the Indian Stamp Act at 0.0020\% ($0.20$ bps) on the buy side.
    \item \textbf{Exchange Transaction Charges (NSE):} Blended rate of 0.0019\% ($0.19$ bps) levied on both buy and sell legs ($0.38$ bps round-trip).
    \item \textbf{SEBI Turnover Fees:} Fixed regulatory levy of 0.0001\% ($0.01$ bps) on both legs ($0.02$ bps round-trip).
    \item \textbf{Goods and Services Tax (GST):} 18.0\% tax levied on brokerage and exchange transaction fees ($\approx 0.12$ bps round-trip).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Execution Slippage and Liquidity Microstructure}
Following the market microstructure frameworks of \citet{roll1984simple}, \citet{lesmond1999new}, and \citet{amihud2002illiquidity}, real-world execution friction consists of both explicit tax charges and implicit market impact slippage. To accurately reflect institutional execution constraints without assuming infinite liquidity \citep{frazzini2018trading}, we model execution slippage using Average Daily Traded Value (ADTV) liquidity tiers:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Slippage}_{\text{tier}} = \begin{cases}
        2.0 \text{ bps per leg (}4.0\text{ bps round-trip)} & \text{if ADTV} \ge \text{Rs.~100 Cr} \\
        5.0 \text{ bps per leg (}10.0\text{ bps round-trip)} & \text{if Rs.~25 Cr} \le \text{ADTV} < \text{Rs.~100 Cr} \\
        10.0 \text{ bps per leg (}20.0\text{ bps round-trip)} & \text{if ADTV} < \text{Rs.~25 Cr}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The total institutional round-trip transaction cost $C_{i,t}$ for stock $i$ at date $t$ is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    C_{i,t} = \text{STT}_t + \text{Stamp} + 2 \times (\text{NSE}_{\text{fee}} + \text{SEBI}_{\text{fee}}) + \text{GST} + 2 \times \text{Slippage}_i
\end{equation}
Under baseline market liquidity, all-in round-trip costs average $6.5$ basis points prior to October 2024, and $7.3$ basis points post-October 2024. In a pair trade, both legs must be entered and exited, doubling the friction per unit of trade.
