\section{Institutional Framework and Transaction Costs}
\label{sec:microstructure}

Running a quantitative arbitrage book in Mumbai is fundamentally different from running one in New York or London. Because retail and institutional short selling in the cash equity segment is held back by an illiquid borrow mechanism, quant managers seeking market neutrality almost always turn to single-stock futures on the National Stock Exchange.

\subsection{Statutory Indian Derivative Taxes}
In Western markets, institutional commissions are often fractions of a basis point, and high-volume firms even earn maker-taker rebates. In India, however, government taxes and exchange fees are fixed by statute and apply equally to all participants \citep{bose2007securities}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Securities Transaction Tax (STT):} Levied strictly on the sell side of futures trades. From 2010 through September 2024, this was 0.0125\% ($1.25$ bps). On October 1, 2024, SEBI raised it by 60\% to 0.0200\% ($2.00$ bps) to curb speculative retail trading in derivatives.
    \item \textbf{Stamp Duty:} Imposed nationwide under the Indian Stamp Act at 0.0020\% ($0.20$ bps) on the buy leg.
    \item \textbf{Exchange Turnover Fees (NSE):} Blended charge of 0.0019\% ($0.19$ bps) on both buy and sell orders ($0.38$ bps round-trip).
    \item \textbf{SEBI Regulatory Fees:} A turnover fee of 0.0001\% ($0.01$ bps) on both sides ($0.02$ bps round-trip).
    \item \textbf{Goods and Services Tax (GST):} An 18.0\% tax levied on brokerage and exchange fees, adding about $0.12$ bps round-trip.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Slippage and Full Implementation Costs}
Beyond taxes, any real trading strategy faces execution slippage \citep{roll1984simple,lesmond1999new,amihud2002illiquidity,frazzini2018trading}. We model realistic slippage using Average Daily Traded Value (ADTV) tiers:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Slippage}_{\text{tier}} = \begin{cases}
        2.0 \text{ bps per leg (}4.0\text{ bps round-trip)} & \text{if ADTV} \ge \text{Rs.~100 Cr} \\
        5.0 \text{ bps per leg (}10.0\text{ bps round-trip)} & \text{if Rs.~25 Cr} \le \text{ADTV} < \text{Rs.~100 Cr} \\
        10.0 \text{ bps per leg (}20.0\text{ bps round-trip)} & \text{if ADTV} < \text{Rs.~25 Cr}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Putting all components together, total round-trip cost $C_{i,t}$ for stock $i$ on date $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
    C_{i,t} = \text{STT}_t + \text{Stamp} + 2 \times (\text{NSE}_{\text{fee}} + \text{SEBI}_{\text{fee}}) + \text{GST} + 2 \times \text{Slippage}_i
\end{equation}
For an average large-cap stock, baseline round-trip friction was roughly $6.5$ basis points prior to October 2024, rising to $7.3$ basis points afterward. Crucially, a pair trade involves two distinct stocks. Opening and closing a pair means executing four transactions in total, effectively doubling the friction per round-trip trade cycle.

\subsection{The Structural Failure of the Cash Short-Selling Mechanism (SLB)}
A defining structural feature of the Indian equity market is the near-total absence of a viable cash market short-selling mechanism. In developed markets such as the United States, prime brokers maintain extensive securities lending desks that allow hedge funds and market makers to borrow shares seamlessly against cash or Treasury collateral. The borrow fee (repo rate or special fee) fluctuates continuously based on supply and demand, rarely exceeding 25 to 50 basis points per annum for general collateral equities (D'Avolio 2002).

In India, the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) introduced the Securities Lending and Borrowing (SLB) framework in 1997 and comprehensively overhauled it in 2008 through the clearing corporations (NSE Clearing Limited). Despite repeated regulatory attempts to promote SLB, market participation remains virtually non-existent for single stocks. Institutional custodians face prohibitive operational and tax hurdles: institutional domestic mutual funds and insurance companies face rigid mandate restrictions on lending portfolio assets, contracts operate under rigid monthly tenures with fixed settlement cycles rather than continuous open recalls, and lenders bear significant corporate action and tax withholding uncertainties. Consequently, borrow liquidity is absent for over 95\% of listed equities. For quantitative fund managers seeking two-sided market neutrality, shorting in the cash market is operationally impossible, leaving exchange-traded single-stock futures on the National Stock Exchange (NSE) as the sole viable avenue for taking short positions.


\subsection{Granular Decomposition of Statutory Derivative Friction}
The reliance on exchange-traded futures introduces a unique and immutable cost structure. Unlike US exchanges where high-frequency and quantitative market makers receive liquidity maker rebates and negotiate institutional commissions down to fractions of a basis point, trading on Indian exchanges is subject to compulsory statutory levies enacted by the central and state governments (Bose 2007). These levies are levied on gross contract turnover and cannot be negotiated or compressed through order routing algorithms.

The most punitive component of this structure is the Securities Transaction Tax (STT), originally introduced under Chapter VII of the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2004. For equity futures, STT is levied on the sell side of every transaction. Historically set at 0.0125\% (1.25 basis points) of contract value, SEBI and the Ministry of Finance enacted a major 60\% rate increase effective October 1, 2024, raising STT on futures sales to 0.0200\% (2.00 basis points) in an effort to curb surging derivative turnover. In addition, the Indian Stamp Act (amended in 2019 and effective July 2020) imposes a harmonized nationwide stamp duty of 0.0020\% (0.20 basis points) on the buy leg of all derivatives trades.

Beyond government taxes, participants pay NSE exchange turnover charges (blended at 0.0019\% or 0.19 bps on both legs, totaling 0.38 bps round-trip), SEBI regulatory turnover charges (0.0001\% per leg, or 0.02 bps round-trip), and Goods and Services Tax (GST) levied at 18.0\% on all brokerage and exchange fees (contributing approximately 0.12 bps round-trip). Because statistical arbitrage inherently requires four individual transaction legs per pair trade cycle—opening a long position, opening a short position, closing the long position, and closing the short position—the cumulative statutory and execution friction is effectively quadrupled relative to a single-stock directional holding. For an institutional trader, statutory friction establishes a non-negotiable floor of roughly 6.5 to 7.3 basis points per round-trip trade, making rapid portfolio churn financially lethal.

To illustrate this compounded friction, consider a representative pair trade involving two large-cap equities, Stock A and Stock B, with an average position holding period of 8 trading days. Entering the trade entails buying Stock A futures and shorting Stock B futures; exiting entails reversing both legs. The buy legs incur stamp duty and exchange fees, while the sell legs incur the full statutory STT levy. Even before accounting for bid-ask spread crossing and liquidity slippage, statutory levies absorb 14.6 basis points of gross spread convergence across the four legs. For a strategy targeting annualized gross returns of 10\% to 15\% with an average holding period of 6 to 10 days, statutory taxes alone deplete more than 100\% of the strategy's gross profit margin.
