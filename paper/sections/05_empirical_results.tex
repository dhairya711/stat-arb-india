In frictionless terms (.0\times$ cost multiplier), all three quantitative frameworks generate economically substantial and statistically robust profits. Strategy B (PCA-OU mean-reversion) delivers a gross annualized return of $+17.91\%$ with a Sharpe ratio of $+1.74$ and a Newey-West $-statistic of $+7.49$ ( < 10^{-12}$). Strategy A (classical distance pairs) yields $+9.50\%$ gross annualized return with a Sharpe ratio of $+0.95$ ($-stat $= +4.11$). Strategy C (walk-forward machine learning) achieves $+8.75\%$ gross return with a Sharpe ratio of $+1.00$ ($-stat $= +4.05$).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig1_cumulative_returns_gross_net.png}
\caption{Cumulative Wealth Index: Gross (.0\times$) vs. Institutional Net (.0\times$) Across Strategies (2010--2025). Shaded regions represent market stress periods.}
\label{fig:fig1}

However, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}, the introduction of baseline institutional transaction costs (.0\times$) reveals a dramatic divergence between theoretical alpha and investable returns---what we term the \textit{Gross Alpha Illusion}. Under statutory Indian derivative taxes and realistic liquidity-tiered slippage, Strategy A suffers an annualized net return of $-25.16\%$ (Net Sharpe $=-2.52$, Newey-West $-statistic $=-10.89$). Strategy B drops to $-16.75\%$ net per annum (Net Sharpe $=-1.63$, $-stat $=-7.00$). Strategy C, despite using non-linear predictive features, experiences the steepest degradation to $-25.91\%$ net per annum (Net Sharpe $=-2.96$, $-stat $=-12.00$). 

The stationary block bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals for the net Sharpe ratio strictly exclude zero across all models: $[-2.96, -2.05]$ for Strategy A, $[-2.06, -1.17]$ for Strategy B, and $[-3.51, -2.42]$ for Strategy C. This establishes conclusively that the negative net performance is an invariant structural feature of the strategy mechanics rather than an artifact of outlier market shocks.

\label{fig:fig2}

Figure~\ref{fig:fig2} plots the historical underwater drawdown profiles for each strategy under the baseline institutional cost model. While gross strategies display modest maximum peak-to-trough drawdowns between $-12.34\%$ and $-14.57\%$ with swift recoveries, the net portfolios experience monotonic capital decay. Strategy A incurs a maximum net drawdown of $-98.57\%$, Strategy B reaches $-95.06\%$, and Strategy C suffers $-98.72\%$. Because daily trading friction acts as an ongoing negative drift, the portfolios fail to establish new high-water marks, remaining underwater for over 90\% of the 16-year sample.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

To assess temporal stability, Figure~\ref{fig:fig3} displays rolling 12-month net Sharpe ratios. For all three strategies, the rolling Sharpe ratio resides below zero in over 92\% of rolling windows. Even during favorable market phases (such as the low-volatility bull runs of 2014 and 2017), the rolling net Sharpe rarely breaches the $+0.5$ threshold, confirming that market environment shifts cannot overcome the frictional hurdle.

Key insights emerge from the sub-period decomposition:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Crisis Resilience of PCA Factor Neutrality:} During the extreme volatility of the February--March 2020 COVID-19 liquidity shock, Strategy B experienced an annualized net return of $-0.56\%$ (Sharpe $-0.06$), significantly outperforming Strategy A ($-57.45\%$) and Strategy C ($-69.86\%$). Because Strategy B explicitly strips out systematic market and sector components via principal component regression, it avoids the catastrophic divergence that afflicts naive distance pairs when macro correlations break down.
    \item \textbf{Impact of the October 2024 STT Hike:} In the post-reform period from October 1, 2024 to December 31, 2025, when STT on futures sales rose from 1.25 bps to 2.00 bps, net annualized performance deteriorated across the board. Strategy A's net return dropped to $-31.30\%$ (Sharpe $-3.19$), and Strategy B's net return fell from its SP2 average of $-12.97\%$ to $-21.40\%$ (Sharpe $-2.05$). This empirical shift directly validates our hypothesis that statutory tax hikes disproportionately penalize high-frequency statistical arbitrageurs.
\end{enumerate}

By computing the root of the net Sharpe ratio function $\text{SR}(M) = 0$, we obtain the exact breakeven cost multipliers:
\begin{equation}
    M_{\text{breakeven}} = \begin{cases}
        0.48\times \text{ (equivalent to } 7.9 \text{ bps round-trip, Strategy A)} \\
        0.90\times \text{ (equivalent to } 14.9 \text{ bps round-trip, Strategy B)} \\
        0.44\times \text{ (equivalent to } 7.3 \text{ bps round-trip, Strategy C)}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

As highlighted in Figure~\ref{fig:breakeven}, even in a hyper-optimized proprietary trading environment with execution costs halved to .5\times$ (approx.~8.5 bps round trip), Strategy A and Strategy C remain deeply unprofitable ($-7.83\%$ and $-8.58\%$ annualized). Only Strategy B barely achieves break-even ($+0.58\%$ return, Sharpe $+0.06$). Given that statutory non-negotiable government levies account for roughly 4.0 to 5.0 bps of fixed costs per round-trip trade, an institutional trader cannot compress total transaction friction below .50\times$, rendering standalone daily pairs and machine learning statistical arbitrage infeasible in the Indian market.

This demonstrates that the negative net returns are not caused by directional market drops or factor tilts, but represent pure transaction friction drag superimposed on a market-neutral baseline.
