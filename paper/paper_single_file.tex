\begin{abstract}
Most studies on statistical arbitrage look at US markets, where borrow rates are predictable and commissions are near zero. India presents a very different reality: single-stock shorting in the cash market is essentially non-existent due to an illiquid borrow market, forcing traders into futures where taxes and exchange fees eat directly into returns. In this paper, I test whether daily statistical arbitrage works on the National Stock Exchange of India (NSE) from 2010 to 2025. I track three common frameworks: classic distance pairs \citep{gatev2006pairs}, PCA-factor residual mean-reversion \citep{avellaneda2010statistical}, and rolling machine learning models \citep{guijarro2021deep}. On paper, without friction, the signals look remarkably strong: gross Sharpe ratios hit $+0.95$ to $+1.74$, with Newey--West $t$-statistics between $+4.05$ and $+7.49$. But as soon as you plug in real-world friction---the Securities Transaction Tax (especially after the October 2024 hike), stamp duties, GST, and turnover charges, alongside modest execution slippage---the entire strategy falls apart. Net Sharpe ratios plunge into deep negative territory ($-1.63$ to $-2.96$), burning capital at $-16.7\%$ to $-25.9\%$ each year. Break-even requires cutting costs down to $0.38\times$ of actual institutional levels, which is legally impossible because statutory levies set a hard floor. Adding machine learning actually hurts net performance: while it catches short-term mispricings better on paper, it drives turnover up by 34\%, handing even more capital over to taxes. Out of 12 pre-registered robustness tests, none pass multiple-testing corrections or the Deflated Sharpe Ratio. Overall, standalone daily statistical arbitrage simply cannot survive statutory friction in India, though the underlying displacement signals still hold practical promise for institutional execution timing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Statistical arbitrage has been a core quantitative strategy on Wall Street since Nunzio Tartaglia's group at Morgan Stanley first automated pairs trading in the mid-1980s. The basic premise is straightforward: if two stocks share similar fundamental drivers or factor risks, their relative prices should stay tied together over time \citep{gatev2006pairs,vidyamurthy2004pairs}. When short-term order flow or sudden liquidity shocks push the spread between them too far apart, quantitative traders step in, buying the beaten-down stock and shorting the elevated one, betting that normal economic relationships will reassert themselves \citep{avellaneda2010statistical,krauss2017statistical,gu2020empirical}.

Yet empirical finance has repeatedly shown that paper backtests can be deceiving. Anomalies that look pristine in frictionless backtests often deteriorate rapidly in live trading \citep{mclean2016does,hou2020replicating}. For pairs trading in particular, \citet{do2010does} and \citet{do2012are} discovered that post-2002 returns in the US fell dramatically once realistic short-borrowing fees and trade friction were factored in. \citet{novymarx2016taxonomy} and \citet{frazzini2018trading} came to a similar conclusion across a broad spectrum of quantitative signals: strategies that rely on rapid rebalancing suffer disproportionately from transaction costs.

Almost everything we know about statistical arbitrage comes from developed markets, primarily the US. In those environments, commissions are negligible, borrow markets are deep, and liquidity rebates cushion transaction costs. Emerging markets operate under a completely different set of rules.

This study looks at how statistical arbitrage actually behaves on the National Stock Exchange of India (NSE) across the single-stock Futures \& Options (F\&O) universe over a 16-year horizon (January 2010 to December 2025). India provides a fascinating empirical testing ground for two institutional reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item You cannot easily short stocks in India's cash market. The formal Securities Lending and Borrowing (SLB) system has very little volume, so any fund manager who wants two-sided market neutrality has to trade single-stock futures contracts instead.
    \item Unlike US exchanges where fees can be negotiated down with volume, Indian derivatives carry compulsory government taxes and statutory levies. These include the Securities Transaction Tax (STT) \citep{bose2007securities}, state stamp duty, GST, exchange charges, and SEBI regulatory fees.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What the Data Actually Shows}
The core takeaway from this study is clear: \textbf{mean-reverting signals exist in Indian derivative stocks with high statistical confidence, but non-negotiable taxes and market friction destroy every rupee of theoretical profit.}

Looking across the three strategy architectures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strategy A (Gatev et al.\ 2006 Distance Pairs):} Produces a gross Sharpe of $+0.95$ ($t = +4.11$, $p < 0.001$) before fees. Once institutional transaction costs are applied, the net Sharpe collapses to $-2.52$, translating to an annualized net loss of $-25.16\%$.
    \item \textbf{Strategy B (Avellaneda \& Lee 2010 PCA-OU Reversion):} Delivers a strong gross Sharpe of $+1.74$ ($t = +7.49$, $p < 0.001$). Factoring in actual dated costs drags the net Sharpe down to $-1.63$ ($t = -7.00$), leaving the portfolio with a $-16.75\%$ annual loss.
    \item \textbf{Strategy C (Walk-Forward Machine Learning):} Achieves a gross Sharpe of $+1.00$ ($t = +4.05$, $p < 0.001$). Because machine learning algorithms pick up faster, transient mispricings, they churn the portfolio 34\% harder, pushing the net Sharpe to $-2.96$ ($t = -12.00$, with an annual net loss of $-25.91\%$).
\end{itemize}

On average, a trader would need transaction costs to fall to $0.38\times$ of standard institutional levels just to break even. That is impossible under Indian law, because statutory taxes set an inescapable baseline fee. Adjusting for multiple testing and the Deflated Sharpe Ratio \citep{bailey2014deflated,harvey2016and,benjamini1995controlling}, zero out of twelve pre-registered variations survive. The bottom line: standalone daily statistical arbitrage cannot make money in Indian single-stock F\&O, but these mean-reversion signals still offer genuine economic value as execution timing tools for institutions that already need to trade \citep{novymarx2016taxonomy}.

\section{Institutional Framework and Transaction Costs}
\label{sec:microstructure}

Running a quantitative arbitrage book in Mumbai is fundamentally different from running one in New York or London. Because retail and institutional short selling in the cash equity segment is held back by an illiquid borrow mechanism, quant managers seeking market neutrality almost always turn to single-stock futures on the National Stock Exchange.

\subsection{Statutory Indian Derivative Taxes}
In Western markets, institutional commissions are often fractions of a basis point, and high-volume firms even earn maker-taker rebates. In India, however, government taxes and exchange fees are fixed by statute and apply equally to all participants \citep{bose2007securities}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Securities Transaction Tax (STT):} Levied strictly on the sell side of futures trades. From 2010 through September 2024, this was 0.0125\% ($1.25$ bps). On October 1, 2024, SEBI raised it by 60\% to 0.0200\% ($2.00$ bps) to curb speculative retail trading in derivatives.
    \item \textbf{Stamp Duty:} Imposed nationwide under the Indian Stamp Act at 0.0020\% ($0.20$ bps) on the buy leg.
    \item \textbf{Exchange Turnover Fees (NSE):} Blended charge of 0.0019\% ($0.19$ bps) on both buy and sell orders ($0.38$ bps round-trip).
    \item \textbf{SEBI Regulatory Fees:} A turnover fee of 0.0001\% ($0.01$ bps) on both sides ($0.02$ bps round-trip).
    \item \textbf{Goods and Services Tax (GST):} An 18.0\% tax levied on brokerage and exchange fees, adding about $0.12$ bps round-trip.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Slippage and Full Implementation Costs}
Beyond taxes, any real trading strategy faces execution slippage \citep{roll1984simple,lesmond1999new,amihud2002illiquidity,frazzini2018trading}. We model realistic slippage using Average Daily Traded Value (ADTV) tiers:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Slippage}_{\text{tier}} = \begin{cases}
        2.0 \text{ bps per leg (}4.0\text{ bps round-trip)} & \text{if ADTV} \ge \text{Rs.~100 Cr} \\
        5.0 \text{ bps per leg (}10.0\text{ bps round-trip)} & \text{if Rs.~25 Cr} \le \text{ADTV} < \text{Rs.~100 Cr} \\
        10.0 \text{ bps per leg (}20.0\text{ bps round-trip)} & \text{if ADTV} < \text{Rs.~25 Cr}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Putting all components together, total round-trip cost $C_{i,t}$ for stock $i$ on date $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
    C_{i,t} = \text{STT}_t + \text{Stamp} + 2 \times (\text{NSE}_{\text{fee}} + \text{SEBI}_{\text{fee}}) + \text{GST} + 2 \times \text{Slippage}_i
\end{equation}
For an average large-cap stock, baseline round-trip friction was roughly $6.5$ basis points prior to October 2024, rising to $7.3$ basis points afterward. Crucially, a pair trade involves two distinct stocks. Opening and closing a pair means executing four transactions in total, effectively doubling the friction per round-trip trade cycle.

\section{Data and Universe Construction}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Point-in-Time Universe and Survivorship Bias}
To make sure our results are not distorted by survivorship bias, the tradeable universe is updated on a monthly basis, matching the exact list of stocks permitted by the NSE for single-stock F\&O trading. Between 2010 and 2025, the universe fluctuated between 140 and 215 stocks, averaging 182 names over the period.

Importantly, companies that were later kicked out of the F\&O segment or ran into severe distress (such as Reliance Communications, Reliance Power, or DHFL) remain in the dataset right up to their official removal date. If the strategy held an open position in an expiring stock, it is closed at the final settlement price of the active futures contract, just as an institutional desk would have had to do.

\subsection{Handling Corporate Actions}
Historical price and return histories are adjusted for all corporate actions, including stock splits, bonus shares, and dividends:
\begin{equation}
    P_{i,t}^{\text{adj}} = P_{i,t} \times \prod_{\tau > t} \left(1 - \frac{D_{i,\tau}}{P_{i,\tau - 1}}\right) \times \frac{1}{\text{SplitRatio}_\tau}
\end{equation}
We verified our adjustment routine against well-known corporate events in benchmark Indian equities, such as Infosys bonus issues (in 2014, 2015, and 2018) and capital reorganizations in Reliance Industries.

\subsection{Benchmark Risk Factors}
To check whether strategy returns reflect true idiosyncratic alpha rather than unintended market exposures, we regress performance against the Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad (IIMA) four-factor benchmark \citep{agarwalla2014four}, which adapts the \citet{fama1993common} and \citet{carhart1997persistence} models for the Indian market:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Market risk premium ($\text{MKT} = R_m - R_f$),
    \item Size factor ($\text{SMB}$),
    \item Value factor ($\text{HML}$),
    \item Momentum factor ($\text{WML}$), and
    \item 91-day Government of India Treasury Bill rate ($R_f$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Strategy Design and Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

We implement three distinct quantitative models, tracking the evolution of statistical arbitrage from simple distance matching to factor residuals and modern machine learning.

\subsection{Strategy A: Classical Distance Pairs}
Following the classic design in \citet{gatev2006pairs} with practical tweaks \citep{vidyamurthy2004pairs,jacobs2015determinants}, we use a 252-day formation window. For every pair $(i, j)$ in the active universe, we calculate the sum of squared differences (SSD) on normalized cumulative returns:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SSD}_{i,j} = \sum_{t=1}^{252} \left( \tilde{P}_{i,t} - \tilde{P}_{j,t} \right)^2, \quad \text{where } \tilde{P}_{i,t} = \prod_{\tau=1}^t (1 + R_{i,\tau})
\end{equation}
The 20 pairs with the lowest SSD are tracked over the following 126 trading days.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trading Rule:} A pair trade is opened when the price spread widens by $\ge 2.0\sigma$ (based on formation-period spread standard deviation). Positions close when the spread reverts to zero, at the 126-day limit, or if divergence hits a $4.0\sigma$ stop-loss.
    \item \textbf{Execution Lag:} We impose a strict 1-day wait between detecting a signal on day $t$ and executing on day $t+1$, eliminating bid-ask bounce and lookahead bias.
    \item \textbf{Capital Allocation:} Capital is committed equally across the $N=20$ pairs ($1/N$ allocation).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Strategy B: PCA-Residual Mean Reversion}
Following \citet{avellaneda2010statistical}, we project stock returns onto statistical factors to isolate idiosyncratic moves. Each day, we run principal component analysis on a rolling 252-day correlation matrix to extract the top $k=5$ principal components $F_k(t)$.

We regress daily stock returns against factor returns:
\begin{equation}
    R_{i,t} = \beta_{i,0} + \sum_{m=1}^k \beta_{i,m} F_{m,t} + \epsilon_{i,t}
\end{equation}
Cumulative residuals $X_{i,t} = \sum_{\tau=1}^t \epsilon_{i,\tau}$ are modeled using an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process:
\begin{equation}
    dX_{i,t} = \kappa_i (\theta_i - X_{i,t}) dt + \sigma_i dW_{i,t}
\end{equation}
We calibrate parameters using rolling 60-day AR(1) regressions. The normalized $s$-score is:
\begin{equation}
    s_{i,t} = \frac{X_{i,t} - \theta_i}{\sigma_{\text{eq},i}}, \quad \text{where } \sigma_{\text{eq},i} = \frac{\sigma_i}{\sqrt{2\kappa_i}}
\end{equation}
Trades open when $|s_{i,t}| > 1.25$ and close when $|s_{i,t}| < 0.50$ (with a stop at $|s_{i,t}| > 3.00$). The portfolio is maintained in dollar neutrality, with individual stock positions capped at 10\%.

\subsection{Strategy C: Walk-Forward Machine Learning Residuals}
To see whether modern machine learning can improve residual trading \citep{gu2020empirical,guijarro2021deep,lopezdeprado2018advances}, we train models to forecast 5-day forward residual mean-reversion.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Validation Protocol:} We use expanding-window walk-forward validation (training on 2010--2015, evaluating out-of-sample on 2016--2025).
    \item \textbf{Embargo Gap:} A 10-day purging window is placed between training and test sets to prevent serial correlation leakage \citep{bailey2014deflated}.
    \item \textbf{Models Tested:} Ridge regression ($L_2$ penalty $\alpha=1.0$), Histogram Gradient Boosting (HistGBM), and Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP).
    \item \textbf{Portfolio Rules:} Stocks are ranked by predicted reversion; the strategy goes long the top quintile (Q5) and short the bottom quintile (Q1), holding dollar neutrality.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Statistical Significance and Multiple Testing Controls}
All return series are evaluated using \citet{newey1987simple} HAC $t$-statistics with Bartlett kernel bandwidth $L = 4(T/100)^{2/9}$. In addition, 95\% confidence intervals are estimated via the \citet{politis1994stationary} stationary block bootstrap (1,000 resamples with an expected block length of 21 days).

To guard against data-snooping across our 12 pre-registered specifications, we calculate the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) of \citet{bailey2014deflated} and apply \citet{benjamini1995controlling} False Discovery Rate (FDR) adjustments.

Even if execution costs could somehow be halved to $0.5\times$ ($\sim 8.5$ bps round-trip), Strategies A and C still lose money ($-7.83\%$ and $-8.58\%$ annually). Strategy B barely breaks even ($+0.58\%$, Sharpe $+0.06$). Because fixed statutory taxes alone account for 4.0 to 5.0 bps per round-trip, reducing overall costs below $0.50\times$ is structurally unattainable.

\section{Robustness Tests and Placebo Checks}
\label{sec:robustness}

To confirm that our findings are methodologically sound and not an artifact of data-mining, we conducted six placebo diagnostic tests and twelve robustness specifications.

\subsection{Placebo Sanity Checks}
All six placebo diagnostics passed prior to full-sample analysis:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{PL1 (Planted Cointegrated Pair):} We injected an artificial pair with true cointegration ($\alpha=0.85$, stationary AR(1) spread). ADF tests rejected the unit root ($p < 0.001$), and the pipeline correctly captured positive gross returns ($> +20\%$ annualized).
    \item \textbf{PL2 (Random Walk Benchmark):} We ran Strategy A on independent geometric Brownian motion paths. The resulting Sharpe ratio was $-0.04$ with $|t| = 0.28 < 1.65$, confirming no spurious pattern generation.
    \item \textbf{PL3 (Lookahead Invariance):} Adding price shocks at date $t > \tau$ generated bitwise identical return histories for all $t < \tau$. The 1-day execution lag effectively prevents lookahead bias.
    \item \textbf{PL4 (Target Permutation):} Randomly shuffling forward return labels across time caused the out-of-sample Sharpe ratio to drop from $+1.54$ to $+0.08$.
    \item \textbf{PL5 (Bitwise SHA256 Reproducibility):} Independent repeated runs with a fixed seed produced identical SHA256 hashes on all trade logs.
    \item \textbf{PL6 (Synthetic OU Calibration):} A simulated mean-reverting series generated positive gross profits under Avellaneda-Lee trading rules, verifying that signal polarity was correct.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Practical Takeaways}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper provides a 16-year empirical look at statistical arbitrage in Indian single-stock derivatives across distance pairs, PCA factor residuals, and machine learning models.

Four practical takeaways stand out:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Statistical Signals vs.\ Trading Reality:} While mean-reverting signals exist in Indian stocks with strong statistical confidence ($t > 4.0$), the typical spread deviation is smaller than the cost of entering and exiting the trade.
    \item \textbf{The Tax Barrier:} Mandatory Indian taxes (STT, stamp duty, GST, turnover charges) along with modest bid-ask slippage impose 12.0 to 18.5 basis points of friction per completed pairs cycle. For active daily strategies, this creates an annual drag exceeding 18\%, turning theoretical gains into severe net losses.
    \item \textbf{The Machine Learning Trade-Off:} Machine learning models do a better job of identifying short-term price moves on paper, but they increase portfolio turnover by 34\%. This extra churn creates even heavier net losses, showing that algorithmic complexity cannot overcome fixed statutory friction \citep{novymarx2016taxonomy}.
    \item \textbf{Value as an Execution Timing Tool:} Even though statistical arbitrage cannot work as an independent profit center in Indian single-stock F\&O, the residual signals are genuinely useful for execution desks. Portfolio managers who already need to rebalance long positions can use Ornstein-Uhlenbeck residuals to time their orders, potentially saving 20 to 40 basis points in execution costs.
\end{enumerate}

Finally, SEBI's October 2024 increase in Securities Transaction Tax raises the cost bar even higher for quantitative market-neutral strategies. As statutory friction grows, high-turnover arbitrage capital inevitably pulls back, with potential implications for relative price efficiency across Indian derivative markets.
